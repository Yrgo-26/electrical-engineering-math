\facitavsnitt{c8}{Chapter 8}{Functions}
\begin{facit}

\svar{1.1} \sv{a} A function: each $U$ gives exactly one $I$.
\sv{b} A function: all the $U$ values are different ($I = 250$ occurring twice is allowed).
\sv{c} Not a function: $U = 1$ gives both $I = 2$ and $I = 5$.

\svar{1.2} \sv{a} $D_U = [-40, 125]\,^{\circ}\text{C}$, $V_U = [0.1, 1.75]\,\text{V}$
\sv{b} $D_U = [0, \infty)\,\text{W}$, $V_U = [0, \infty)\,\text{V}$
\sv{c} $D_P = [-20, 20]\,\text{V}$, $V_P = [0, 40]\,\text{W}$

\svar{1.3} \sv{a} $k = 1.5\,\Omega/{}^{\circ}\text{C}$, $m = 100\,\Omega$
\sv{b} $137.5\,\Omega$

\svar{2.1} \sv{a} $C(10) \approx 2950\,\text{mAh}$: after $10$ years about $74\,\%$ of the capacity remains.
\sv{b} After about $7.3$ years.

\svar{2.2} \sv{a} A power function, $P(I) = 50I^2$ (a quadratic function).
\sv{b} $P(0.5) = 12.5\,\text{W}$, $P(2) = 200\,\text{W}$
\sv{c} $D_P = [0, 3]\,\text{A}$, $V_P = [0, 450]\,\text{W}$

\svar{2.3} \sv{a} The voltage falls by $3\,\text{V}$ every second; a constant change gives a
linear relationship.
\sv{b} $U(t) = -3t + 12$
\sv{c} $t = 5\,\text{s}$. No: the voltage across a discharging capacitor does not become negative
but levels out towards $0\,\text{V}$, so the model holds only within the measured interval
$0 \leq t \leq 4\,\text{s}$.

\svar{3.1} \sv{a} $10\,\text{V}$ \sv{b} $4\,\text{V}$ \sv{c} $0\,\text{W}$ \sv{d} $45\,\text{W}$ \sv{e} $3\,\text{A}$ \sv{f} $2\,\text{A}$

\svar{3.2} \sv{a} $1.5\,\text{A}$ \sv{b} $I = 6\,\text{A}$: the terminal voltage is zero, the battery is short-circuited.
\sv{c} $2\,\text{A}$ (mathematically also $-2\,\text{A}$, the same current in the opposite direction)
\sv{d} No: $I^2 \geq 0$, so $P(I) \geq 0$. \sv{e} $48\,\Omega$

\svar{3.3} \sv{a} $R \neq 0$; reasonable $R > 0$ \sv{b} $R \neq -2$; reasonable $R \geq 0$
\sv{c} $P \geq 0$; the same \sv{d} $f \neq 0$; reasonable $f > 0$

\svar{3.4} \sv{a} $[0, 40]\,\text{W}$ \sv{b} $[0, 12]\,\text{V}$ \sv{c} $[0, 10]\,\text{V}$
\sv{d} $[-40, 125]\,^{\circ}\text{C}$

\svar{3.5} \sv{a} $U(I) = -0.5I + 9$ \sv{b} $U(T) = 0.01T + 0.5$
\sv{c} $R(T) = 0.385T + 100$ \sv{d} $I(t) = 2$, a constant function.

\svar{3.6} \sv{a} The slope: how much $U$ changes when $I$ increases by $1\,\text{A}$.
\sv{b} The intercept with the $y$-axis, $U(0) = m$.
\sv{c} Decreasing, since $k = -0.5 < 0$.
\sv{d} $I = 24\,\text{A}$, the short-circuit current.
\sv{e} $m = 12\,\text{V}$ is the open-circuit voltage, the terminal voltage at $I = 0$. The slope
$k = -0.5$ is minus the internal resistance, $R_{\text{i}} = 0.5\,\Omega$.

\svar{3.7} \sv{a} Both are $90\,\text{W}$: the square removes the minus sign.
\sv{b} $9$ times larger. \sv{c} It becomes a quarter.
\sv{d} $2.5\,\text{W}$ and $10\,\text{W}$: the power becomes $4$ times larger.

\svar{3.8} \sv{a} $5$, $10$ and $40\,\text{mV}$ \sv{b} $100$, $50$ and $12.5\,\text{mV}$
\sv{c} $u$ is increasing and $g$ decreasing. The base decides: $a > 1$ gives an increasing function,
$0 < a < 1$ a decreasing one.
\sv{d} $C = 5\,\text{mV}$ and $C = 100\,\text{mV}$; $C$ is the initial value, the signal before
the first stage.

\svar{3.9} \sv{a} $1.05$ \sv{b} $0.80$ \sv{c} $f(n) = 2 \cdot 0.7^n\,\text{V}$
\sv{d} $0.686\,\text{V}$ \sv{e} After $2$ stages, when it is $0.98\,\text{V}$.

\svar{3.10} \sv{a} $f$: the difference between adjacent values is constant, $2$.
\sv{b} $g$: the ratio between adjacent values is constant, $2$.
\sv{c} $f(x) = 2x + 3$ \sv{d} $g(x) = 3 \cdot 2^x$ \sv{e} $f(10) = 23$, $g(10) = 3072$

\svar{3.11} \sv{a} $I = 3\,\text{A}$: the short-circuit current.
\sv{b} $I = 0$ and $I = 6\,\text{A}$: no power is delivered without current, nor at a short
circuit, when the whole voltage lies across the internal resistance.
\sv{c} $t = 12\,\text{s}$: then the voltage is down to $0\,\text{V}$.

\svar{3.12} \sv{a} $0\,\text{V}$, $6\,\text{V}$ and $10.8\,\text{V}$
\sv{b} $V_U = [0, 10.8]\,\text{V}$
\sv{c} $x = 2\,\text{k}\Omega$
\sv{d} No: the denominator $1 + x$ is always greater than the numerator $x$, so
$U < 12\,\text{V}$. The voltage approaches $12\,\text{V}$ but never reaches it.

\svar{3.13} \sv{a} $10\,\text{V}$ \sv{b} $u(2) \approx 3.68\,\text{V}$,
$u(4) \approx 1.35\,\text{V}$ \sv{c} Decreasing. \sv{d} $V_u = (0, 10]\,\text{V}$
\sv{e} No: $e^{-t/2} > 0$ for all $t$, so the voltage approaches $0\,\text{V}$ but never becomes
exactly zero.

\svar{3.14} \sv{a} False: $I^2 \geq 0$, so the power is never negative.
\sv{b} False: $R = 0$ gives division by zero.
\sv{c} False: a zero requires $U = 0$, but $U(2) = 5$.
\sv{d} True: a function gives exactly one $I$ for each $U$.
\sv{e} True: $u(0) = 3 \cdot 2^0 = 3\,\text{V}$.

\svar{4.1} \sv{a} $f(0) = -50\,^{\circ}$C, $f(0.8) = 30\,^{\circ}$C, $f(5) = 450\,^{\circ}$C
\sv{b} $-50$, $0$, $50$, $\ldots$, $450\,^{\circ}$C: the temperature rises by $50\,^{\circ}$C per
half volt, the slope $k = 100$.
\sv{c} $x = \dfrac{f + 50}{100}$. $30\,^{\circ}$C gives $0.8$ V, and
\code{voltage(temperature(0.8))} gives $0.8$ back, since the functions are each other's
inverses.

\svar[Test yourself]{T} \sv{1} For example $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ with $D_f = [0, \infty)$: the square
root of a negative number is not defined. (Or $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$, where $x = 0$ gives division by
zero.)
\sv{2} $f(2) = 9$, $f(-1) = 6$

\end{facit}
