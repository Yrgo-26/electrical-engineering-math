\section{Definition}
\label{c6:sec:definition}
A \textbf{quadratic equation} (an equation of the second degree) is an equation of the form
\[
  ax^2 + bx + c = 0, \quad a \neq 0.
\]
The coefficients $a$, $b$ and $c$ are real numbers. The equation can have \textbf{0, 1 or 2 real
roots}.

\begin{exempel}
$2x^2 - 3x - 5 = 0$ is a quadratic equation with $a = 2$, $b = -3$ and $c = -5$.
\end{exempel}

\section{Methods of solution}
\label{c6:sec:losningsmetoder}

\subsection{The factorisation method}
\label{c6:sec:faktorisering}
If $ax^2 + bx + c$ can be factorised as $a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)$, the roots are $x = r_1$ and
$x = r_2$.

\begin{exempel}
Solve $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$. Factorise:
\[
  (x - 2)(x - 3) = 0
\]
The roots are given by $x - 2 = 0$ or $x - 3 = 0$:
\[
  x_1 = 2, \quad x_2 = 3
\]
\end{exempel}

\begin{exempel}[Difference of two squares]
Solve $x^2 - 9 = 0$.
\[
  (x + 3)(x - 3) = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = -3 \text{ or } x = 3
\]
\end{exempel}

\subsection{The PQ-formula}
\label{c6:sec:pq}
The PQ-formula, the name Swedish schools give the quadratic formula in this form, applies to
equations in \emph{normalised} form $x^2 + px + q = 0$, that is, with $a = 1$:
\[
  x = -\frac{p}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2 - q}
\]

\begin{exempel}
Solve $x^2 - 4x + 3 = 0$. Here $p = -4$ and $q = 3$:
\begin{gather*}
  x = \frac{4}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{4}{2}\right)^2 - 3} = 2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 3} = 2 \pm 1 \\*
  x_1 = 3, \quad x_2 = 1
\end{gather*}
\end{exempel}

The expression under the root sign is called the \textbf{discriminant} and decides the number of
real roots: if it is positive there are two roots, if it is zero they coincide in a double root,
and if it is negative there are no real roots.

The PQ-formula can also be used by moving everything except $x^2$ to the right-hand side, so that
the equation has the form $x^2 = px + q$. Then $p$ and $q$ go into the formula with the signs they
have on the right-hand side:
\[
  x^2 = px + q \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = \frac{p}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2 + q}
\]

\begin{exempel}[Alternative form]
Solve $x^2 + 2x + 2 = 26$. Move everything except $x^2$ to the right-hand side:
\[
  x^2 = -2x + 24
\]
Here $p = -2$ and $q = 24$:
\begin{gather*}
  x = \frac{-2}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{-2}{2}\right)^2 + 24} = -1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 24} = -1 \pm 5 \\*
  x_1 = 4, \quad x_2 = -6
\end{gather*}
\end{exempel}

The two forms give the same calculation; $p$ and $q$ simply have opposite signs. Choose the form
you prefer and stick to it.

If $a \neq 1$, first divide every term by $a$, so that the equation is normalised.

\begin{exempel}
Solve $2x^2 + 5x - 3 = 0$. Divide by $2$:
\[
  x^2 + 2.5x - 1.5 = 0
\]
Here $p = 2.5$ and $q = -1.5$:
\begin{gather*}
  x = -1.25 \pm \sqrt{1.25^2 + 1.5} = -1.25 \pm \sqrt{3.0625} = -1.25 \pm 1.75 \\*
  x_1 = 0.5, \quad x_2 = -3
\end{gather*}
\end{exempel}

\subsection{Completing the square}
\label{c6:sec:kvadratkomplettering}
Every quadratic equation can be written in the form $(x + k)^2 = m$ by ‘completing the square’.
For $x^2 + bx + c = 0$ it goes like this:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Move the constant: $x^2 + bx = -c$.
  \item Add $\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2$ to both sides.
  \item The left-hand side becomes a perfect square.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{exempel}
Solve $x^2 + 6x + 5 = 0$.
\[
  x^2 + 6x = -5
\]
Add $\left(\frac{6}{2}\right)^2 = 9$:
\[
  x^2 + 6x + 9 = -5 + 9 = 4
\]
\[
  (x + 3)^2 = 4 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x + 3 = \pm 2
  \quad \Rightarrow \quad x_1 = -1, \quad x_2 = -5
\]
\end{exempel}

\section{Applications in electrical engineering}
\label{c6:sec:tillampning}

\subsection{Current from power and resistance}
\label{c6:sec:strom}
The power in a resistor $R$ is given by $P = RI^2$. Given $P$ and $R$, the current $I$ can be
calculated:
\[
  RI^2 = P \quad \Rightarrow \quad I^2 = \frac{P}{R} \quad \Rightarrow \quad I = \sqrt{\frac{P}{R}}
\]
This is a \emph{pure} quadratic equation, with no linear term.

\begin{exempel}
With $P = 8\,\text{W}$ and $R = 50\,\Omega$,
\[
  I = \sqrt{\frac{8}{50}} = \sqrt{0.16} = 0.4\,\text{A}.
\]
\end{exempel}

\subsection{Resistance from a series connection}
\label{c6:sec:serie}
Two resistors $R_1$ and $R_2$ are connected in series with $R_1 + R_2 = 10\,\Omega$ and
$R_1 \cdot R_2 = 24\,\Omega^2$. Solve the first equation for $R_2 = 10 - R_1$ and substitute it
into the second:
\[
  R_1(10 - R_1) = 24 \quad \Rightarrow \quad R_1^2 - 10R_1 + 24 = 0
\]
The PQ-formula gives
\[
  R_1 = 5 \pm \sqrt{25 - 24} = 5 \pm 1.
\]
The root $R_1 = 6\,\Omega$ gives $R_2 = 10 - 6 = 4\,\Omega$. The other root, $R_1 = 4\,\Omega$,
gives the same pair in the opposite order, that is,
\[
  R_1 = 6\,\Omega, \quad R_2 = 4\,\Omega.
\]

\section{Extra: the ABC-formula}
\label{c6:sec:abc}
The ABC-formula (the quadratic formula) is an alternative to the PQ-formula, included as optional
extra material. It gives the same roots, but can be used directly on $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ without
first normalising the equation:
\[
  x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}
\]

\begin{exempel}
Solve $2x^2 + 5x - 3 = 0$. Here $a = 2$, $b = 5$ and $c = -3$:
\[
  x = \frac{-5 \pm \sqrt{5^2 - 4 \cdot 2 \cdot (-3)}}{2 \cdot 2} = \frac{-5 \pm \sqrt{49}}{4}
  = \frac{-5 \pm 7}{4} \quad \Rightarrow \quad x_1 = 0.5, \quad x_2 = -3
\]
The roots are the same as the PQ-formula gave for the same equation in \secref{c6:sec:pq}.
\end{exempel}
