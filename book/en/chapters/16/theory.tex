\section{Euler's formula}
\label{c16:sec:euler}
\textbf{Euler's formula} connects complex exponents and trigonometry:
\[
  e^{j\delta} = \cos\delta + j\sin\delta
\]
This means that every complex number can be written in \textbf{Euler form}:
\[
  z = \abs{z} \cdot e^{j\delta}
\]

\section{Three forms of complex numbers}
\label{c16:sec:former}
A complex number can be written in three ways, and each form has its strengths.

\begin{booktable}{@{}p{7em}p{6em}L@{}}
  \thead{Form} & \thead{Notation} & \thead{Comment} \\
  \midrule
  Rectangular & $x + jy$ & Best for addition and subtraction \\
  Polar & $\abs{z}\,\angle\,\delta$ & Clear geometric interpretation \\
  Euler form & $\abs{z}e^{j\delta}$ & Best for multiplication and division \\
\end{booktable}

\noindent\textbf{Multiplication} in polar form:
\[
  z_1 \cdot z_2 = \abs{z_1}\abs{z_2}\,\angle\,(\delta_1 + \delta_2)
\]
\textbf{Division} in polar form:
\[
  \frac{z_1}{z_2} = \frac{\abs{z_1}}{\abs{z_2}}\,\angle\,(\delta_1 - \delta_2)
\]

\section{Phasors}
\label{c16:sec:fasor}
A \textbf{phasor} is a complex number that represents a sinusoidal signal. A voltage
$u(t) = \abs{U}\sin(\omega t + \delta)$ is represented by the phasor
\[
  U = \abs{U}\,\angle\,\delta = \abs{U}e^{j\delta}.
\]
Phasors simplify calculations with sine waves in electric circuits: adding sine waves reduces to
adding complex numbers.

\section{Converting between Euler form and the time domain}
\label{c16:sec:omskrivning}
From phasor to time domain:
\[
  U = \abs{U}e^{j\delta} \quad \Rightarrow \quad u(t) = \abs{U}\sin(\omega t + \delta)
\]
From time domain to phasor:
\[
  u(t) = \abs{U}\sin(\omega t + \delta) \quad \Rightarrow \quad U = \abs{U}\,\angle\,\delta
\]

\section{Why Euler form?}
\label{c16:sec:varfor}
In calculations, the polar form $\abs{U}\,\angle\,\delta$ is usually the simplest to write. Euler
form explains why it works.

\paragraph{Multiplication and division follow from the laws of exponents} The exponents are added
in multiplication and subtracted in division:
\[
  \abs{z_1}e^{j\delta_1} \cdot \abs{z_2}e^{j\delta_2} = \abs{z_1}\abs{z_2}\,e^{j(\delta_1 + \delta_2)}
\]
This is why the angles are added when two numbers are multiplied in polar form.

\paragraph{The frequency can be left out} All voltages and currents in a circuit have the same
frequency. The only things that differ between them are the amplitude and the phase, and these are
contained in the phasor $U = \abs{U}e^{j\delta}$. We therefore calculate with phasors and put
$\omega t$ back only when the answer is to be written as $u(t) = \abs{U}\sin(\omega t + \delta)$.

\subsection{Differentiation with Euler's formula}
This is beyond the course, but it
shows why Euler's formula makes differentiation and integration so much simpler. We write the
complex voltage in rectangular form using Euler's formula:
\[
  u(t) = \abs{U}\cos(\omega t + \delta) + j\abs{U}\sin(\omega t + \delta)
\]
We differentiate with the chain rule, where the inner derivative is $(\omega t + \delta)' = \omega$:
\begin{align*}
  u'(t) &= -\abs{U}\sin(\omega t + \delta) \cdot (\omega t + \delta)'
           + j\abs{U}\cos(\omega t + \delta) \cdot (\omega t + \delta)' \\
        &= -\omega\abs{U}\sin(\omega t + \delta) + j\omega\abs{U}\cos(\omega t + \delta)
\end{align*}
Since $j^2 = -1$, $-\omega\abs{U}\sin(\omega t + \delta)$ can be written as
$j\omega \cdot j\abs{U}\sin(\omega t + \delta)$, so $j\omega$ can be factored out:
\[
  u'(t) = j\omega\bigl(\abs{U}\cos(\omega t + \delta) + j\abs{U}\sin(\omega t + \delta)\bigr)
        = j\omega \cdot u(t)
\]
In Euler form, the same differentiation takes a single step. With
$u(t) = \abs{U}e^{j(\omega t + \delta)}$, the inner derivative is
$\bigl(j(\omega t + \delta)\bigr)' = j\omega$:
\[
  u'(t) = \bigl(j(\omega t + \delta)\bigr)' \cdot \abs{U}e^{j(\omega t + \delta)}
        = j\omega \cdot \abs{U}e^{j(\omega t + \delta)} = j\omega \cdot u(t)
\]
The result is the same, but in Euler form it is enough to multiply by $j\omega$. Integration works
the same way in reverse: integrating corresponds to dividing by $j\omega$.

\subsection{Impedance of an inductor and a capacitor}
With the result above, the impedances are easy
to derive. The voltage across an inductor is $u = L\frac{di}{dt}$. With the current
$i(t) = \abs{I}e^{j(\omega t + \delta)}$, the differentiation above gives
$\frac{di}{dt} = j\omega \cdot i(t)$, so
\[
  u(t) = L\frac{di}{dt} = L \cdot j\omega \cdot i(t) \quad \Rightarrow \quad
  Z_L = \frac{u(t)}{i(t)} = j\omega L
\]
The current through a capacitor is $i = C\frac{du}{dt}$, and in the same way
$\frac{du}{dt} = j\omega \cdot u(t)$, so
\[
  i(t) = C\frac{du}{dt} = C \cdot j\omega \cdot u(t) \quad \Rightarrow \quad
  Z_C = \frac{u(t)}{i(t)} = \frac{1}{j\omega C}
\]
The impedances of the inductor and the capacitor thus follow directly from differentiation
corresponding to multiplication by $j\omega$. Impedances are covered further in
Chapter~\ref{c17:ch}.

\section{Worked examples}
\label{c16:sec:typexempel}

\begin{exempel}[Multiplication in Euler form]
Calculate $z_1 \cdot z_2$ when $z_1 = 2e^{j\pi/6}$ and $z_2 = 3e^{j\pi/4}$. Give the answer in both
Euler form and rectangular form.

The magnitudes are multiplied and the exponents added:
\[
  z_1 \cdot z_2 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot e^{j(\pi/6 + \pi/4)} = 6e^{j5\pi/12}
\]
The angle is $\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{2\pi}{12} + \frac{3\pi}{12} = \frac{5\pi}{12}$,
that is, $75^{\circ}$. Euler's formula gives the rectangular form:
\[
  6e^{j5\pi/12} = 6\left(\cos 75^{\circ} + j\sin 75^{\circ}\right)
    \approx 6(0.259 + j0.966) \approx 1.55 + j5.80
\]
\end{exempel}

\begin{exempel}[Euler form to rectangular form]
Write $u(t) = 3e^{j(100\pi t - \pi/4)}\,\text{V}$ in rectangular form at $t = 0$.
\begin{align*}
  u(0) &= 3e^{-j\pi/4}
        = 3\left(\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) + j\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\right) \\
       &= 3\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - j\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) \approx 2.12 - j2.12\,\text{V}
\end{align*}
\end{exempel}

\begin{exempel}[Phasor to time domain]
The phasor $I = 5 - j4\,\text{mA}$ belongs to the angular frequency $\omega = 100\pi\,\text{rad/s}$.
Write $i(t)$ in Euler form.
\begin{gather*}
  \abs{I} = \sqrt{25 + 16} = \sqrt{41} \approx 6.40\,\text{mA}, \\
  \delta = \arctan\left(\frac{-4}{5}\right) \approx -38.7^{\circ} \approx -0.675\,\text{rad}, \\
  i(t) = 6.40 \cdot e^{j(100\pi t - 0.675)}\,\text{mA}.
\end{gather*}
\end{exempel}
